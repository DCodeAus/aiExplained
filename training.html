Part 2 - Training: How AI Learns

A language model starts as a blank slate that knows nothing. Training is how it goes from random noise to writing poetry. The surprising part: the whole process is built on one simple game - guess the next token.

The prediction game

Cover the end of this sentence and guess the last word: "She poured milk into her bowl of ___". You almost certainly said "cereal". You've read and heard enough English that your brain has absorbed its patterns.

That, at its core, is what an LLM learns to do. During training, the model is shown billions of text snippets with the ending hidden and asked: what token comes next? At first its guesses are random garbage. Each time it guesses wrong, its internal settings - called parameters or weights - are nudged slightly in the direction that would have made the right answer more likely. Repeat this trillions of times, and the model gets astonishingly good at the game.

It's like a student doing millions of fill-in-the-blank flashcards. No one ever hands them a grammar book or an encyclopaedia. But to get really good at predicting the blank, they're forced to absorb grammar, facts, reasoning styles and even humour - because all of those help predict what comes next.

What "learning" physically means

A model is essentially an enormous mathematical formula with billions of adjustable dials (the parameters). Modern models have anywhere from a few billion to over a trillion of them. Training is the process of turning those dials, tiny nudge by tiny nudge, until the formula's predictions match real text well.

Importantly, the training text itself is not stored inside the model. What's stored is the dial settings. That's why a model can't quote its training data back verbatim on demand - it learnt patterns and associations, not a filing cabinet of documents.

Common confusion Chatting with an AI does not train it. Training happens in advance, in a data centre, over weeks or months. When you chat, the model's dials are frozen - it's only using what it already learnt. (Companies may later use conversations to train future models, but the one you're talking to isn't learning from you live.)

The three stages of building a chatbot

Raw next-token prediction gives you a model that's knowledgeable but wild - ask it a question and it might just continue with more questions, because that's a plausible continuation. Turning it into a helpful assistant takes further stages:

Stage 1: Pre-training. The next-token game, played on a colossal amount of text. This is where the model absorbs language, facts, and reasoning patterns. It's the most expensive stage by far - think thousands of specialised computers running for months.

Stage 2: Fine-tuning (instruction tuning). The model is trained on curated examples of conversations: a question, followed by a genuinely helpful answer. This teaches it the format of being an assistant.

Stage 3: Learning from human feedback. Humans compare pairs of model answers and pick the better one. The model is adjusted to produce more of what people prefer. This is often called RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), and it's a big part of why modern chatbots feel polite and cooperative.

The secret ingredient: attention

One question we've dodged so far: how does the model use the earlier words to predict the next one? Consider: "The trophy wouldn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What does "it" refer to? You instantly know it's the trophy - because you weighed every word in the sentence against every other word.

That's essentially what attention does - the mechanism at the heart of the Transformer architecture (the "T" in GPT, invented at Google in 2017). For every token it processes, the model calculates how much each previous token matters right now, and focuses on the relevant ones. Predicting after "it was too", the model's attention lights up on "trophy" and "fit", not on "the". Stack dozens of layers of this, and the model can track who did what to whom across pages of text.

Reading the word "it", your eyes don't literally re-read the sentence - but your mind flicks back to "trophy" automatically. Attention is that flick-back, done with arithmetic, for every token, at every layer, all at once.

This is why the Transformer replaced everything that came before it: older designs read text one word at a time and slowly forgot the beginning. Attention lets the model consider the whole context simultaneously - which is exactly what made today's large context windows (Part 3) possible.

Why models make things up

Now you can see where hallucinations come from. The model's fundamental job is to produce plausible text, not true text. If you ask about something it saw a lot of in training, plausible and true usually coincide. If you ask about something obscure - a small-town restaurant, a niche court case, a paper that doesn't exist - the model still generates something plausible-sounding, because generating text is all it can do. It has no built-in way to say "no data found" like a database would.

Key takeaway LLMs learn by playing "guess the next token" on vast amounts of text, then get polished into assistants with human feedback. They store patterns, not documents - which makes them fluent, flexible, and occasionally confidently wrong.
