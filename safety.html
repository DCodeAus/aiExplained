Part 5 - Safe & Honest Use

The most common beginner mistake isn't misunderstanding tokens - it's pasting the wrong thing into a chat box. This page covers what should never leave your machine, and how to use AI while studying without cheating yourself.

What not to paste into an AI

When you type something into a chatbot, it travels to a company's servers, is processed there, and is handled according to their terms - which you probably haven't read, and which may allow conversations to be reviewed or used to improve future models. So the golden rule:

The golden rule Never paste anything into an AI that you wouldn't be comfortable emailing to a stranger at that company.

In practice, keep these out of public chatbots:

Don't pasteWhy
Passwords, banking details, API keys, MFA codesCredentials are never needed for the AI to help you - anything that asks for them is a red flag.
Other people's personal data (names + contact details, health info, HR complaints)It's not yours to share. In Australia, mishandling it can also breach the Privacy Act - and it's simply unfair to the person.
Confidential work material (client lists, unreleased financials, internal strategy)Many workplaces prohibit it outright; people have been disciplined for it. Use your employer's approved AI tools if they exist - that's exactly what enterprise plans (like Copilot 365) are for.
Anything under an NDA, or legal/medical documents about othersSame principle: confidentiality obligations don't pause because the recipient is a robot.
Your full identity documents (passport, licence, Medicare numbers)Identity-theft material. If you need help with a form, retype the question, not the document.

Two softeners worth knowing. Most paid and enterprise plans offer settings to opt out of training on your conversations - worth switching on (in ChatGPT and Claude it's in the data/privacy settings). And you can often redact as you go: replace real names with "Person A", real figures with round numbers. The AI helps just as well with the shape of a problem as with the real data.

Treat a chatbot like a talkative consultant in a café. Brilliant to bounce ideas off - but you wouldn't slide your passport, your mate's medical file, or the company's unreleased accounts across the table, because you don't control where that information goes next.

Using AI while studying - without cheating yourself

If you're a student, AI presents a genuine fork in the road, and it's worth being honest about it. The same tool can make you dramatically better at your subject or quietly ensure you never learn it.

The test is simple: after the AI interaction, can you do something you couldn't do before? If yes, you used it as a tutor. If no - if the work is done but you're unchanged - you used it as a ghostwriter, and you're the one who paid for the privilege.

Tutor mode (builds you)Ghostwriter mode (hollows you out)
"Explain why this loop never terminates. Don't fix it - give me a hint.""Fix my code." (paste, copy, submit, learn nothing)
"Quiz me on inheritance vs composition until I get five right.""Write my OOP assignment in a casual student style."
"I wrote this paragraph - critique the argument like a tough marker.""Write the paragraph. Make it sound like me."
"Explain this compiler error like I'm new to C#.""Do questions 1-8. Show working so it looks hand-done."

Three practical rules for study:

Struggle first. Attempt every problem for at least fifteen minutes before asking AI - the struggle is where learning physically happens. Then ask for a hint, not an answer. Close the loop. After AI explains something, put it away and redo the problem from scratch. If you can't, you haven't learnt it yet. Know your institution's policy. Universities (Swinburne included) publish rules on acceptable AI use per unit, and they differ between units. Submitting AI-written work as your own is academic misconduct everywhere - but many units explicitly allow AI for explanation and feedback. When in doubt, ask the convenor; "I didn't know" doesn't survive a misconduct hearing.

The long game Here's the irony: the better you understand your subject, the more powerful AI becomes in your hands - you write sharper prompts, catch its mistakes, and push it further (everything in Part 3). Skipping the learning to use AI now makes you permanently worse at using AI later.

A note on scams and fakes

One more safety item, because it's increasingly unavoidable: the same technology on this site generates convincing fake voices, photos, videos and messages. Assume that "urgent" voice message from a family member, or a video of a public figure saying something outrageous, could be synthetic. Verify through a second channel (call them back on their known number) before acting on anything involving money or credentials. Healthy scepticism about AI output - the theme of this whole site - now applies to your inbox too.
