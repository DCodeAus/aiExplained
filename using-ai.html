Part 3 - Using AI Well

You now know what's under the bonnet. This page covers the practical side: how to talk to an AI, what its limits are, and the handful of settings and concepts that make you dramatically more effective with it.

Prompts: you get out what you put in

Everything you type to an AI is called a prompt. Because the model is a next-token predictor, your prompt is quite literally the text it's continuing from - so the quality and clarity of your prompt shapes the answer more than most people realise.

Compare these two requests:

❌ "Write about dogs."

✅ "Write a friendly 150-word introduction to choosing a first dog, aimed at apartment renters in Australia. Mention two suitable breeds and one common mistake."

The second prompt pins down the audience, length, tone, and content. Vague in, vague out. A few habits that consistently improve results:

Give context. Who are you, what's this for, what have you already tried? Show examples. "Here's one I like - write in this style." Ask for a format. A table, a list, a step-by-step plan. Iterate. The first answer is a draft, not a verdict - reply with "shorter", "more formal", "explain point 2 more" and it improves. Ask it to reason step by step for tricky problems - working through steps before answering measurably improves accuracy.

The context window: AI's working memory

A model can only "see" a limited amount of text at once - its context window, measured in tokens (from Part 1). Everything in the current conversation - your messages, its replies, any documents you've pasted - must fit in this window.

A context window is like a whiteboard. Everything relevant to your conversation gets written on it. It's big - modern models can hold hundreds of pages - but it's finite. When it fills up, the oldest writing gets wiped to make room. And when you start a new conversation, the whiteboard is erased completely.

This explains two everyday experiences: very long conversations can make the model "forget" things said early on, and a new chat knows nothing about your previous chats (unless the product adds a memory feature on top, which some now do).

Temperature: the creativity dial

Remember from Part 2 that the model produces probabilities for the next token, not one fixed answer. Temperature controls how it picks from those probabilities. Low temperature: nearly always pick the most likely token - predictable, consistent, sometimes bland. High temperature: give less-likely tokens a real chance - varied, creative, sometimes unhinged.

🔬 Interactive: the temperature dial

The sentence is "The weather today is …". Drag the slider and watch how the odds of each next word shift, then press "Generate" a few times at different temperatures.

At very low temperature you'll get "sunny" almost every time. At high temperature, anything can happen. Chat products usually run somewhere in the middle - which is why asking the same question twice gives different answers.

🎮 Game: hot or cold?

I've secretly set the temperature to either very low (0.1) or very high (1.9) and generated four endings for "The weather today is …". Can you tell which setting it was? Guess right 3 times in a row to win points.

Hallucinations: confident nonsense

As covered in Part 2, models generate plausible text, not guaranteed-true text. A hallucination is when the output is fluent, confident, and wrong - an invented citation, a fake court case, a plausible-sounding statistic.

Practical defences:

Treat specifics with suspicion. Names, dates, numbers, quotes, citations and URLs are the highest-risk outputs - verify anything that matters. Prefer tools with search or citations for factual questions, and click through to the sources. Ask the model to say when it's unsure - imperfect, but it helps. Never rely on an unverified answer for medical, legal, or financial decisions.

Rule of thumb Use AI like a brilliant, fast, occasionally overconfident colleague: superb for drafts, ideas, explanations and summaries - but you sign off on the final answer, so you check the facts.

What AI is great at (and not)

Plays to its strengthsFights its weaknesses
Drafting and rewriting text, changing tonePrecise arithmetic on its own (without tools)
Summarising and explaining documents you provideFacts about very recent events (without search)
Brainstorming, naming, outliningObscure niche facts - high hallucination risk
Explaining concepts at any level ("explain like I'm 10")Knowing what happened in your previous chats
Writing and explaining codeGuaranteed correctness of anything, unreviewed
Key takeaway Clear, specific prompts; awareness of the context window; healthy scepticism about facts. Master those three and you're ahead of most AI users.
