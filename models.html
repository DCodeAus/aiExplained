Models & Makers: Who Built What, and Which Tool to Pick

The famous CEOs are the salespeople. This page covers the researchers whose work actually made modern AI possible, then compares three tools you're likely to meet - Microsoft 365 Copilot, Claude, and OpenAI Codex - including plans, limits, and how to set each one up.

Date stamp Plans, prices and setup steps below are accurate as of 31 October 2026. This industry moves fast - prices are in USD and change often, so treat this as a snapshot and check the official pages (linked in each section) before paying for anything.

Where "AI" came from: a brief history

People often guess the 1960s, but the term is a little older: "artificial intelligence" was coined in 1955-56 by John McCarthy for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College, USA. McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon and Nathaniel Rochester gathered a small group with a gloriously optimistic premise - that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it". They thought a summer might crack it. It took seventy years and counting.

YearWhat happened
1950Alan Turing publishes "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", proposing the imitation game (now the "Turing test"): can a machine converse indistinguishably from a human?
1956The Dartmouth workshop - the term "artificial intelligence" is born, and AI becomes a field.
1958Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron - the first trainable artificial neuron, ancestor of every neural network including LLMs.
1960s - 70sEarly optimism (chatbot ELIZA, 1966), then the first "AI winter" - funding collapses when promises outrun results. A boom-and-bust pattern the field repeats.
1986Backpropagation popularised by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams - the training algorithm (the "nudge the dials" step from Part 2) still used today.
1997IBM's Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov - brute-force search, not learning, but a landmark.
2012AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton) demolishes the ImageNet image-recognition contest using a deep neural network on gaming GPUs - the deep-learning era begins.
2017Google's Transformer paper (below) - the architecture of every modern LLM.
2022ChatGPT launches and reaches 100 million users in two months - AI goes mainstream.

Two things worth noticing in that timeline: nearly seventy years passed between the idea and the product in your pocket - and the same three ingredients unlocked it in the end: better algorithms (Transformers), more data (the internet), and more computing power (GPUs).

The actual makers (not the front men)

Sam Altman didn't invent GPT any more than a record label CEO writes the songs. Here's the research lineage that matters:

The foundation everyone builds on

The Transformer (2017) - the architecture inside every model on this page - came from eight Google researchers: Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Niki Parmar, Jakob Uszkoreit, Llion Jones, Aidan Gomez, Łukasz Kaiser and Illia Polosukhin, in the paper "Attention Is All You Need". All eight later left Google, mostly to found their own AI companies (Shazeer co-founded Character.AI; Gomez co-founded Cohere).

The "T" in GPT stands for Transformer. Without this paper, none of the tools below exist.

OpenAI (GPT models, ChatGPT, Codex)

Alec Radford was first author on the original GPT-1 and GPT-2 papers - arguably the single person most responsible for the GPT line existing. Ilya Sutskever (co-founder and long-time chief scientist, previously a deep-learning pioneer under Geoffrey Hinton) set the research direction for years; Jakub Pachocki succeeded him as chief scientist in 2024. The original 2021 Codex model - the ancestor of today's Codex product and of GitHub Copilot - was led by researchers including Mark Chen (now OpenAI's chief research officer) and Wojciech Zaremba. The technique that turned raw GPT into chatty ChatGPT (RLHF, from Part 2) traces heavily to alignment researcher Paul Christiano and colleagues.

Anthropic (Claude)

Anthropic was founded in 2021 by a group who left OpenAI. Dario Amodei is the public face, but the founding technical bench matters more: Tom Brown (lead author of the GPT-3 paper - he built the thing at OpenAI first), Jared Kaplan (the physicist behind "scaling laws" - the discovery that models predictably improve as you make them bigger, which justified the whole industry's spending), Chris Olah (the leading figure in interpretability - the science of looking inside a model to see what it's actually doing), plus Sam McCandlish, Ben Mann, Jack Clark and Daniela Amodei. Claude's signature training method, Constitutional AI, came from Anthropic researchers including Yuntao Bai.

Microsoft Copilot

Here's the twist: Microsoft didn't build the brain. Copilot runs on OpenAI's models (with Microsoft's own and Anthropic's models increasingly mixed in) - Microsoft's engineering contribution is the plumbing that connects those models to your Word documents, emails, spreadsheets and Teams chats (a system it calls the "Copilot orchestrator" with the Microsoft Graph). The Copilot concept was proven by GitHub Copilot (2021), created by GitHub's research arm under Oege de Moor, with Alex Graveley as its principal creator and namer. Microsoft's consumer AI is now led by Mustafa Suleyman (DeepMind co-founder) - though he's arguably a front man too.

Think of it like a band. The CEOs are the managers doing interviews. Vaswani and the Transformer authors wrote the hit song everyone covers. Radford, Brown, and Kaplan are the session musicians who actually recorded the albums. Microsoft, meanwhile, runs the biggest venue and licenses the music.

Getting set up - as of 31 October 2026

Microsoft 365 Copilot

As an individual: sign in at copilot.microsoft.com with any Microsoft account for the free Copilot Chat. For Copilot inside Word/Excel/Outlook, subscribe to Microsoft 365 Premium from your Microsoft account page - the Copilot button then appears in the ribbon of each Office app automatically.

At work/uni: you can't self-serve - an administrator buys Copilot licences in the Microsoft 365 admin centre and assigns one to your account. If the Copilot button is missing from your Office apps, that's the reason: ask your IT admin. Students: check what your university account already includes before paying for anything.

Claude

Chat: go to claude.ai, sign up with email or Google - free tier works immediately, upgrade to Pro in Settings if you hit limits. Mobile and desktop apps are on the usual app stores.

Claude Code (for developers): needs a paid plan (Pro or above) - the free plan doesn't include it. Prefer not to use a terminal? It's built into the Claude desktop app. Otherwise install it with one command - on Mac or Linux run curl -fsSL https://claude.ai/install.sh | bash, on Windows (PowerShell) run irm https://claude.ai/install.ps1 | iex - then open a new terminal, run claude inside a project folder and sign in with your claude.ai account when prompted. Node.js is no longer needed. Pro is enough to learn with; Max or Ultra is for daily heavy use.

OpenAI Codex

Heads up - this changed in July 2026: the standalone Codex app merged into the ChatGPT desktop app. If you find older guides describing a separate Codex app, they're out of date.

Easiest path: install the ChatGPT desktop app (Mac or Windows) from chatgpt.com and sign in - Codex is a mode inside it, next to Chat and Work, on every plan including Free. On paid plans, Codex Cloud can also work on GitHub repositories you connect.

CLI (local, for terminal users): requires Node.js. Run npm install -g @openai/codex, then codex in a project folder and sign in with your ChatGPT account. IDE extensions for VS Code and similar editors work the same way - search "Codex" in the extensions marketplace.

Honest advice for a beginner Start free on all three before paying: free Claude, free ChatGPT/Codex, free Copilot Chat. Pay only when you hit limits doing real work. If you're learning to code, Claude Pro or ChatGPT Plus (both $20/mo) with its coding tool is far better value than Copilot in Microsoft 365, which shines mainly when an employer pays for it.
